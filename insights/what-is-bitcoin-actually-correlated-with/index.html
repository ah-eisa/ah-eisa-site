What Is Bitcoin Actually Correlated With? Gold, Oil, Inflation or Liquidity?

Bitcoin is often described as digital gold and an inflation hedge. The evidence is more nuanced: its relationship with gold, oil, inflation and liquidity changes across market regimes, with liquidity and risk conditions often explaining more than any single commodity comparison.

Bitcoin is often called digital gold.

That description is attractive because the two assets share several characteristics: scarcity, independence from corporate cash flows, and a narrative around protection from monetary debasement.

But a portfolio manager cannot allocate capital on narrative alone.

The more useful question is not whether Bitcoin resembles gold philosophically. It is whether Bitcoin actually behaves like gold when portfolios need it to.

The same question applies to oil and inflation. If Bitcoin is an inflation hedge, should it benefit when an energy shock pushes inflation expectations higher? If it is a monetary hedge, should it move with gold? If neither relationship is stable, what is actually driving it?

The evidence points to a more complicated conclusion:

Bitcoin is not digital gold, digital oil, or a simple inflation hedge. Its portfolio behaviour is regime-dependent, with liquidity, risk appetite, institutional flows and monetary conditions often explaining more than any single commodity relationship.

That distinction matters because an asset's role in a portfolio should be defined by how it behaves, not by the label attached to it.

Bitcoin and gold: similar thesis, different behaviour

Gold has centuries of history as a monetary reserve and defensive asset. Bitcoin has a fixed maximum supply and an increasingly institutional market structure, but its trading history is much shorter.

Their narratives overlap. Their returns do not consistently do so.

Research examining Bitcoin alongside traditional assets finds that the relationship with gold changes substantially over time. A 2026 study using rolling-window information-flow analysis found no stable long-term relationship between Bitcoin and conventional assets. Bitcoin and gold interacted more strongly during particular shifts in investor risk perception, rather than maintaining a permanent connection.

Recent market evidence reinforces the point. Bitcoin can enter periods in which its correlation with gold rises materially. Coin Metrics, for example, observed a 90-day Bitcoin-gold correlation of approximately +0.56 during one 2026 regime. That is meaningful, but it is a regime observation, not proof of a permanent structural relationship.

This is the first important distinction:

Bitcoin can trade like digital gold without always being digital gold.

Gold's defensive characteristics are heavily influenced by real interest rates, the dollar, central-bank demand and safe-haven flows. Bitcoin remains more sensitive to liquidity, positioning, leverage, ETF flows and broader risk appetite.

During acute market stress, that difference becomes important. Investors seeking liquidity may sell Bitcoin alongside equities and other risk assets even while gold benefits from defensive demand.

Oil: correlation is the wrong question

The relationship between Bitcoin and crude oil is even less direct.

A ten-year study of weekly Bitcoin and oil returns covering 2016 to 2026 found no stable return-level relationship. A statistically meaningful positive relationship appeared mainly during 2020 to 2022, when extraordinary monetary easing affected many asset classes simultaneously. The researchers concluded that a shared liquidity factor explained the episode better than a direct oil-to-Bitcoin relationship.

That makes intuitive sense.

Bitcoin does not consume crude oil in a way that creates a fundamental earnings relationship. Nor does a higher oil price mechanically increase Bitcoin's intrinsic cash flow, because Bitcoin has no cash flow.

Oil matters through the macroeconomic chain it can trigger:

Oil shock → inflation pressure → interest-rate expectations → real yields and dollar → financial conditions → liquidity and risk appetite → Bitcoin.

This is particularly relevant in 2026. Energy-market disruptions have repeatedly pushed oil back into the macro conversation. On 23 October, gold was heading for its sharpest weekly decline in more than four months as higher oil prices revived concerns that interest rates could remain restrictive for longer.

Bitcoin faces the same macro transmission mechanism, but with a different risk profile.

A sharp oil rally can therefore coincide with falling Bitcoin, rising Bitcoin, or almost no Bitcoin response at all depending on what the oil move does to expected monetary policy and global liquidity.

The relationship is conditional, not structural.

Is Bitcoin an inflation hedge?

This is where terminology becomes especially important.

There are at least three different ideas that investors often combine under the phrase "inflation hedge":

  1. An asset that rises when current CPI rises.
  2. An asset that protects against unexpected future inflation.
  3. An asset that protects against long-term currency debasement and monetary expansion.

These are not the same thing.

Bitcoin's fixed supply gives it a strong theoretical argument under the third definition. Only 21 million bitcoin can ultimately exist. A fiat monetary system has no comparable hard supply limit.

But that does not mean Bitcoin should rise every time CPI surprises to the upside.

Empirical evidence increasingly makes that distinction clear. S&P Global's 2026 work concluded that Bitcoin is better understood as a potential hedge against long-term currency debasement, rather than a reliable hedge against short-term inflation. The research found no consistent relationship between Bitcoin returns and short-term inflation expectations.

A separate 2026 study covering 2010 to 2025 similarly found that broad liquidity, measured through variables such as M2, had a stronger long-run association with Bitcoin than CPI. CPI itself was not a robust long-run driver once other macro-financial variables were considered.

That is a major difference for asset allocation.

If inflation rises because demand is strong and liquidity remains abundant, Bitcoin may perform well.

If inflation rises because an oil shock forces central banks to maintain restrictive policy, raises real yields and tightens financial conditions, Bitcoin may struggle.

Both environments can produce higher inflation. They do not produce the same investment regime.

Liquidity may be the more useful macro lens

For much of Bitcoin's history, one of the strongest macro narratives has been its relationship with global liquidity.

The mechanism is straightforward. When monetary and financial conditions loosen, capital becomes cheaper, risk appetite increases and scarce financial assets can attract incremental demand. Bitcoin's fixed supply can amplify that demand effect.

State Street Global Advisors noted in 2026 that Bitcoin historically tended to track M2 money supply and that its relationship with equities may itself partly reflect a common liquidity factor.

Academic work also finds a positive long-run association between broad liquidity and Bitcoin while finding much weaker evidence for CPI as a direct driver.

But even the liquidity relationship should not become another simplistic rule.

Fidelity Digital Assets has highlighted a recent divergence between Bitcoin and global M2, despite the historically useful relationship. Institutional adoption, spot ETFs, changes in market structure, derivatives positioning and the composition of liquidity itself can all alter how monetary conditions reach Bitcoin prices.

This is exactly why static correlations are dangerous.

A relationship can be economically useful without being permanent.

Bitcoin increasingly has more than one identity

The institutionalisation of Bitcoin has created an interesting tension.

Its original monetary characteristics have not disappeared. Supply remains constrained. It remains independent of any single government or corporate balance sheet. Investors can still use it as an expression of concern about long-run monetary debasement.

At the same time, its market behaviour increasingly reflects the financial system it was originally designed to sit outside.

Spot ETFs connect Bitcoin more directly to traditional portfolio flows. Institutional ownership creates new sources of demand but also new channels for risk reduction. Derivatives create leverage and liquidation effects. Macro funds can trade Bitcoin alongside equities, currencies and rates.

As a result, Bitcoin can behave like different assets in different regimes:

  • a scarce monetary asset during debasement trades;
  • a high-beta risk asset during liquidity-driven rallies;
  • a source of liquidity during market stress;
  • a diversifier when correlations with equities and gold fall;
  • and occasionally a gold-like asset when monetary credibility becomes the dominant concern.

Trying to force all of those behaviours into one label loses useful information.

What today's market tells us

The current environment offers a useful real-world example.

Oil prices remain elevated amid geopolitical risk, keeping inflation and monetary-policy concerns in focus. On 23 October, gold was around $4,016 per ounce and heading for a weekly decline of roughly 3.5%, according to Reuters, as higher oil prices increased concerns that U.S. rates could stay restrictive for longer.

At roughly the same time, Bitcoin recovered toward $82,000 during Asian trading after earlier weakness, while gold and equity futures also attempted to stabilise.

The point is not the exact daily move. Daily correlations are noisy.

The useful observation is that an inflationary energy shock does not automatically produce a simultaneous rally in either Bitcoin or gold.

Markets first ask a second question:

What does the inflation shock mean for rates, the dollar and liquidity?

That transmission mechanism can matter more than the inflation number itself.

What this means for portfolio construction

For a multi-asset investor, the conclusion should not be that Bitcoin has no diversification value.

It is almost the opposite.

A permanently high correlation with gold or equities would make Bitcoin less interesting as an additional portfolio exposure. The fact that correlations move across regimes means Bitcoin can potentially contribute differentiated return behaviour.

But that benefit comes with an important condition: the allocation must reflect Bitcoin's volatility and unstable correlations.

Historical correlation matrices should therefore be treated as observations, not constants.

When evaluating Bitcoin in a strategic portfolio, I would focus on five questions:

1. What is the investment objective?

Is Bitcoin being used for long-term asymmetric upside, monetary debasement protection, diversification, tactical macro exposure, or some combination of these?

2. What happens in a liquidity contraction?

A hedge that falls alongside risk assets during the moment of stress may still have long-term value, but it should not be modelled as immediate crisis protection.

3. How much volatility can the portfolio absorb?

Position sizing matters more than the narrative. A relatively small Bitcoin allocation can contribute materially to total portfolio risk because Bitcoin volatility remains much higher than traditional asset classes.

4. Are correlations being measured dynamically?

Rolling correlations, drawdown behaviour and conditional correlations during stress periods are more informative than a single full-history correlation coefficient.

5. What assets does Bitcoin actually replace?

This is perhaps the most important portfolio question. A Bitcoin allocation funded from cash has a different effect from one funded from gold, equities or alternatives.

The decision is not simply whether to own Bitcoin. It is what role the allocation is expected to perform and what opportunity cost it creates elsewhere in the portfolio.

The better question

The debate around whether Bitcoin is "digital gold" is useful for explaining the asset to a broad audience, but it is too narrow for institutional portfolio construction.

Bitcoin does not need to behave exactly like gold to have value.

It does not need to rise with oil to respond to an inflationary energy shock.

And it does not need to track CPI month by month for its fixed supply to matter in a world of long-run monetary expansion.

The evidence so far suggests something more nuanced:

Bitcoin is best treated as a distinct macro-sensitive asset whose behaviour changes with the market regime.

Its relationship with gold can strengthen and weaken. Its direct relationship with oil is limited. Its short-term inflation-hedging record is inconsistent. And its historical connection to liquidity appears more meaningful, while still subject to structural change.

For investors, therefore, the better question is no longer:

"Is Bitcoin digital gold?"

It is:

"What role should Bitcoin play in a multi-asset portfolio, and under which macro regime should we expect that role to change?"

That is a much harder question.

It is also the one that matters.
